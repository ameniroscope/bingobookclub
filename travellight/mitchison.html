The Author

Naomi Mitchison

1897 – 1999
◆

Naomi Mitchison (born Naomi Margaret Haldane) was a Scottish novelist and social activist. Born in Edinburgh and raised in Oxford, where her physiologist father John Scott Haldane was a Fellow, she married the barrister Gilbert "Dick" Mitchison — later a Labour MP — in 1916. Through the 1920s and '30s their Hammersmith home was a hub for artists, writers and politicians, from Aldous Huxley and W. H. Auden to E. M. Forster.[1]

Political life

Mitchison joined the Labour Party in 1930 and campaigned actively through the decade — joining a 1932 Fabian expedition to the Soviet Union, and standing (unsuccessfully) for the Scottish Universities in 1935. In 1939 she settled at Carradale and took part in the Scottish renaissance.[1]

Her most significant political trip was to Vienna in February 1934, after the Fascist Dollfuss government crushed the Socialists in the Austrian Civil War.[2] She went to aid the persecuted socialists,[1][5] taking real personal risk — smuggling documents and left-wing refugees out of the country.[3] Her diary preserves a first-hand account of the arrest, trial and execution of the socialist leader Koloman Wallisch.[4]

Her reach extended beyond Europe. In 1934 she introduced the Labour politician Stafford Cripps to Jawaharlal Nehru, and after Indian independence visited the subcontinent to see her daughter Sonia, teaching in Pakistan. In the 1960s the Bakgatla people of Botswana adopted her as a councillor and "mother," and she wrote extensively about them.[1]

Writing

Mitchison worked across an unusually wide range — historical fiction, science fiction, fantasy, children's books, poetry and memoir.[6]

Historical fiction

She began with novels of the classical and pre-classical world. The Conquered (1923) and The Corn King and the Spring Queen (1931) were among her most successful — the latter often ranked among the century's finest historical novels.[10]

Science fiction

Her best-known science fiction is Memoirs of a Spacewoman (1962), the episodic memoir of Mary, a communications specialist who explores alien life across space and time — taking up communication, sexuality, reproduction and the ethics of first contact.[11] Widely seen as an early landmark of feminist SF, it depicted women as professionals and took alien sexuality — and a woman's right to choose the father of her children — seriously, well ahead of its time.[12][13] Later came Solution Three (1975), on a future that answers overpopulation and conflict with human cloning.[14]

Fantasy and children's writing

She also wrote for younger readers and in fantasy modes — the Arthurian To the Chapel Perilous (1955)[9] and Travel Light (1952), a fairy tale of a girl raised by bears and dragons.[18] Her 1936 collection The Fourth Pig gathers fairy tales, poems and ballads.[8]

Memoir and nonfiction

She wrote extensively about her own life and times: the Vienna Diary (1934), the memoir You May Well Ask (1920–1940, pub. 1979), the wartime diary Among You Taking Notes (1939–1945, pub. 1985), and nonfiction such as The Moral Basis of Politics (1938).[9]
